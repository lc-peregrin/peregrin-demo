# Proof of Onward Travel for Colombia: What You Actually Need Before You Fly

Here's the scenario that ruins a trip before it even starts. You're at the check-in counter in Miami, Panama City or Madrid, boarding pass almost printed, and the agent asks the question that stops everyone in their tracks: "Can I see your onward or return ticket?"

If you booked a one-way flight into Bogotá, Medellín or Cartagena and don't have an answer, some airlines will simply refuse to let you board. Not immigration in Colombia. The airline, at your departure airport, before you've left the ground. Colombia has a real reputation for this, and it catches backpackers and digital nomads off guard because it feels like the kind of thing that "probably won't be checked." Sometimes it isn't. Sometimes it very much is, and you're the one standing at the gate rebooking a flight home you never wanted.

This guide is about making sure that never happens to you.

## What "proof of onward travel" means for Colombia

Proof of onward travel is simply evidence that you don't plan to overstay: a booking showing you'll leave Colombia (or continue to another country) before your permitted stay runs out. It doesn't have to be a return flight to your home country. A bus ticket onward to Ecuador, a flight to Peru, a flight back to wherever you started, all of these can work in principle.

The reason airlines ask for it isn't bureaucratic box-ticking for its own sake. If Colombian immigration turns you away at the border for having no exit plan, the airline that flew you in is usually the one on the hook to fly you back out again, at their own cost. So airline staff at check-in have every incentive to check before you fly, not after you've landed and become someone else's problem.

## The entry and visa picture (verify before you fly)

Colombia allows many nationalities to enter without a visa for tourism, for a set number of days that officials decide on arrival. There's also Check-Mig, Colombia's mandatory online pre-registration for foreign travellers, which is separate from the onward travel question but often gets confused with it.

Here's the important caveat: exact permitted-stay lengths, which nationalities need a visa at all, and how strictly the onward proof rule is enforced all vary by passport and change without much notice. What was true for a Canadian passport last year might not match what's true for a South African or Indian passport today. Don't take any number you read online, including any number that isn't in this article, as gospel. Check Migración Colombia's official guidance and your airline's own conditions of carriage close to your travel date, and if you're arranging a visa through an embassy or consulate, confirm requirements directly with them.

What doesn't change is the underlying logic: airlines and immigration officials both want to see you have a way out of the country before your permitted stay ends.

## Two checkpoints, not one

This is the bit that trips people up. There are two separate moments where onward proof can get checked, and they're not run by the same people with the same incentives.

**Airline check-in**, at your *departure* airport, before you fly to Colombia. This is often the stricter checkpoint, because if Colombian immigration later denies you entry, the airline is generally liable to fly you back at their own expense, sometimes with fines on top. Check-in staff are protecting the airline's bottom line, not making a judgement call about your plans. That's why some travellers sail through with nothing and others get grilled over a one-way ticket on the same route, same day. It depends on the airline, the agent, and how busy the counter is.

**Immigration on arrival** in Colombia. Less consistently enforced, but it's the backstop, and being asked here with no answer is a worse conversation than one at a check-in counter back home.

You can't predict which checkpoint will actually ask, so the only sensible approach is to walk in already able to answer either way.

## Before you fly: a short checklist

1. Check your passport's current visa and permitted-stay status for Colombia at an official source, not a forum thread.
2. Complete Check-Mig if it applies to your entry, separately from your onward proof.
3. Arrange your onward proof before you're standing at the check-in counter: a held reservation, a real onward booking, or a refundable ticket.
4. Save the confirmation as a PDF and keep a printed copy too. Airports don't always have reliable wifi, and a dead phone battery shouldn't be the reason you miss a flight.
5. Have your accommodation details on hand as well. Some agents ask for both together.
6. If your plans change once you're in Colombia, that's fine. This proof is about the day you fly, not a binding itinerary for the rest of the trip.

## FAQ

**Does a held reservation count, or do I need a paid ticket?**
For onward travel proof, a genuine held reservation with a real, checkable booking reference is generally accepted, because it's exactly what's being asked for: evidence you have a way out of the country. You're not required to have already paid for and ticketed a flight you might not use.

**Will Colombian immigration actually ask for this, or is it just the airline?**
Both can, but airline check-in staff at your departure airport tend to be the stricter checkpoint, since they carry the cost if you're later denied entry. Don't assume a relaxed immigration reputation means you're clear at check-in.

**What if I'm travelling overland out of Colombia to Ecuador or Peru later?**
A held reservation fits well here. It gives you something to show now without locking you into a flight when your actual plan is a bus or a booking you haven't arranged yet.

**Can one onward proof cover a multi-country South America trip?**
Usually you need proof of your next exit from Colombia specifically, not a document covering the whole trip.
