# Proof of Onward Travel for Costa Rica (2026): What Actually Gets Checked

Costa Rica has a reputation, and it's earned. Ask around in any hostel in San Jose or on a digital nomad forum and someone will tell you a story about the friend of a friend who got turned away at check-in in their home country because they only had a one-way flight. It happens. Costa Rica is one of the places where proof of onward travel isn't a theoretical rule buried in an immigration handbook, it's something check-in staff and border officers actually ask for, often.

If you're reading this because you're a few days out from a flight and you don't have a return or onward booking sorted, you're not being paranoid. You're being sensible. Let's get you covered properly.

## What "proof of onward travel" means for Costa Rica

Costa Rica generally requires visitors to show that they won't be staying indefinitely. In practice, that means evidence of a flight (or other transport) leaving the country before your permitted stay runs out, whether that's a return home or a onward trip somewhere else.

Entry rules and permitted stay lengths do vary by nationality and change from time to time, so we won't pretend to give you a hard number that applies to everyone reading this. Many visa-exempt nationalities (the US, Canada, the UK, and most of the EU among them) are typically granted a stay of up to 90 days on arrival, but that's a general pattern, not a promise. Always check the current rule for your specific passport before you fly. Costa Rican immigration authorities and your airline are the two sources that actually matter here, not a random forum thread (including, to be fair, this one).

What doesn't vary much is the expectation itself: have something dated, with your name on it, showing you leave before your visa-free stay expires.

## Two checkpoints, not one

This is the part people miss. There isn't one moment where onward travel gets checked, there are two, and they work differently.

**The airline, at check-in.** This is where it bites most often, and it bites hardest. Airlines flying into Costa Rica are contractually on the hook: if they fly you in and Costa Rican immigration decides you shouldn't be admitted, the airline is usually the one who has to fly you home again, at their own cost. That's a real financial liability for them, so check-in agents tend to be strict, sometimes stricter than immigration itself. If your documents look thin, they can simply decline to board you before you ever reach Costa Rican soil.

**Immigration, on arrival.** Costa Rican border officers can also ask to see proof of onward travel when you land, particularly if anything else about your entry looks unusual. It's less commonly enforced than the airline check, but it does happen, and turning up with nothing is a bad gamble.

The upshot: you need something that satisfies whichever gate you hit first, and honestly, something that would satisfy both.

## How to actually satisfy it, ranked

### 1. A genuine, verifiable held reservation

This is the fastest and cheapest fix, and it's the one we'd point a friend toward. A held reservation is a real flight booking under a real PNR (the same record locator system airlines and travel agents use), issued in your name, showing a confirmed onward or return flight. It's not ticketed, meaning you haven't paid the full airfare, but it is checkable: anyone can look it up on the airline's own site or call the airline and confirm it's real. That's what makes it work at check-in. It looks and functions exactly like a normal booking because, structurally, it is one.

### 2. A real onward flight you already booked

If you've genuinely bought a flight out of Costa Rica (say, your next stop is Panama or you're flying home on a set date), that satisfies the requirement outright. The catch is cost and flexibility: you're paying full fare for a ticket you're locking yourself into, on a plan that might still change.

### 3. A fully refundable ticket

Some airlines and travel agents sell tickets you can cancel for a full refund within a set window. These work as proof, but you'll usually pay more upfront than a standard fare, and you have to actually remember to cancel before the refund window closes or you're out the money.

**One thing to never do:** don't edit a PDF, doctor a screenshot, or buy a "proof of onward travel" document from someone offering a booking that isn't real and checkable. Airline staff have seen every version of a faked itinerary, and immigration officers at a border you're trying to enter are the last people you want catching you with one. It can mean denied boarding, denied entry, or worse. A genuine held reservation costs about the same as a coffee habit for a week. It isn't worth the risk of a fake.

## Before you fly: a quick checklist

- Check your nationality's current visa-free stay length for Costa Rica (official sources, not a blog from three years ago).
- Sort your onward travel proof at least a few days before departure, not at the check-in counter.
- Make sure the name on the reservation matches your passport exactly.
- Keep a PDF and a screenshot of the confirmation on your phone, plus a copy in your email, in case you need to show it twice.
- If your plans change after you land, that's fine, you're not obligated to take that exact flight. The reservation just needs to exist at the border.
- Double check your travel insurance covers the dates you'll actually be in the country (more on that below).

## FAQ

**Will a held reservation actually work at Costa Rican check-in?**
Yes. It's a real PNR in the airline's own system, so check-in staff can verify it the same way they'd verify any other booking. It's not a screenshot or a template, it's a genuine reservation.

**Do I need a return flight, or is onward to another country enough?**
Onward is generally fine. Costa Rica (like most countries with this rule) cares that you're leaving before your permitted stay ends, not specifically that you're going home.

**How long before my flight should I sort this?**
As early as makes sense for your planning, but at minimum a day or two before departure so you're not doing it at the airport. A held reservation only takes minutes to arrange, so last-minute isn't a disaster, but don't wait until you're at the check-in desk.

**What if I want to stay longer than my visa-free period allows?**
That's a separate question from proof of onward travel, and it usually involves either an extension process or a border run, depending on current rules. Check official Costa Rican immigration guidance directly, since this changes and matters more than most travel blogs (including this one) can promise to keep perfectly current.

**Is a refundable ticket cheaper than a held reservation?**
Usually not. Refundable fares carry a real premium over standard tickets, and you're on the hook to remember the cancellation. A flat-fee held reservation tends to be the cheaper and lower-effort route for this specific requirement.
