# Proof of Onward Travel for Argentina: What You Actually Need in 2026

Flights booked, a rough plan for Buenos Aires steakhouses and maybe a detour to Patagonia, and then you hit a forum post at midnight saying Argentina might want proof you're leaving again. If you booked one-way, that little knot in your stomach is familiar to basically every long-term traveller who's been here before you.

Take a breath. This is one of the more manageable pre-flight admin tasks you'll deal with, and it usually takes less time than deciding which asado joint to try first. Here's exactly what's being asked for, who tends to ask, and the fastest legitimate way to have it sorted well before you're standing at the check-in counter.

## What proof of onward travel means for Argentina

Proof of onward travel is evidence that you don't plan to stay in Argentina indefinitely. That could be a return flight home, a flight onward to Chile, Uruguay, Brazil, or anywhere else, or another document showing you have a way out before your permitted stay ends.

Many nationalities can enter Argentina for tourism without arranging a visa in advance, and entry is often straightforward for visitors from North America, the EU, the UK, Australia, and a good number of other countries. That's the easy part. The part that catches people out is that visa-free entry isn't the same as no questions asked. Argentina has also, at different points, adjusted things like reciprocity fees for certain nationalities, so it's worth double-checking your specific situation rather than assuming last year's rules still apply. Immigration officers and airline staff can both ask for evidence you intend to leave, particularly if you're arriving one-way, travelling light on documentation, or your ticket pattern looks more like a move than a holiday.

## The entry picture (and why it varies)

Here's the honest version: exact entry requirements for Argentina depend on your nationality, and they shift more often than any of us would like. Most travellers from the US, Canada, the EU, the UK, and Australia get a tourist stay of up to 90 days on arrival without a visa arranged beforehand. Other nationalities face different processes, and stay lengths and fee structures have been known to change with little warning.

We're deliberately not going to hand you a confident table of exact days and fees and pretend it'll still be accurate by the time you land. Argentine immigration policy has moved before, including changes to reciprocity-style entry fees over the years, and it can move again. Check your nationality's current requirements with Argentina's official immigration authority (Dirección Nacional de Migraciones) or your nearest Argentine consulate close to your travel date, and treat this guide as a solid starting point rather than the final word.

What tends not to vary much: if you're flying in on a one-way ticket, there's a real chance someone in the journey, most likely the airline, asks how you plan to leave.

## Two checkpoints, not one

This is the part most people underestimate, so it's worth spelling out clearly.

**Checkpoint one: the airline, at check-in.** Airlines operate under carrier liability rules. If they board a passenger without proof of a legal way to enter and eventually exit Argentina, and that passenger gets turned away at immigration, the airline is often on the hook to fly them home at its own expense, sometimes with a fine attached. That's exactly why airline staff tend to be the stricter gatekeepers. A check-in agent working through a queue isn't going to weigh up the nuance of your travel plans. They want to see a reservation showing you leaving Argentina, and without one, they can deny boarding right there at your departure airport, potentially a full flight away from Buenos Aires.

**Checkpoint two: Argentine immigration, on arrival.** This one is more of a mixed bag. Plenty of travellers land on a one-way ticket and walk straight through. Others get asked directly, and it can come down to the individual officer and how the shift is going.

The practical upshot: even if immigration probably won't ask, the airline checkpoint comes first and is the stricter of the two. Get your proof of onward travel sorted before you're at the airport, not while you're standing in the queue.

### 2. A real onward or return flight you already intend to take

If you already know your next move, say you're heading home in a month or flying onward to Santiago or Montevideo, and you're comfortable locking that fare in now, a genuinely purchased ticket works fine as proof. The trade-off is cost and flexibility: you're paying full fare for a date you might want to shift later, and changing plans once you're mid-trip in South America isn't always cheap or simple.

### 3. A fully refundable ticket

Some travellers book a fully refundable onward ticket, show it at check-in and immigration, then cancel it once they're through. It can work, but refundable fares generally cost considerably more than standard ones, refunds can take weeks to process, and your money sits tied up the whole time in case the cancellation doesn't go smoothly.

### A word of warning

Never try to satisfy this requirement with an edited screenshot, a doctored PDF, or a booking confirmation you've altered yourself. These fall apart the moment anyone checks the actual record locator, and airline staff and immigration officers can and do verify PNRs on the spot. Getting caught with an altered document is a far worse outcome than any onward ticket cost you were trying to avoid. Stick to something genuine and verifiable, always.

## Before you fly to Argentina: a quick checklist

1. Confirm your nationality's current entry requirements and any fees with Argentina's official immigration source or a consulate, close to your departure date.
2. Sort your proof of onward travel before you leave home, not at the airport. A held reservation takes a few minutes.
3. Have a printed or downloaded copy ready to show at check-in, alongside your passport.
4. Check your passport validity against Argentina's current requirements, and carry a photocopy or photo of your passport as backup.
5. Keep your accommodation details on hand too, in case an officer asks where you're staying as a follow-up question.
6. If your plans change once you're in Argentina, that's completely fine. A held reservation was never meant to lock in your real itinerary.

## FAQ

**Do I need a visa to visit Argentina?**
Many nationalities, including US, Canadian, EU, UK, and Australian passport holders, can enter for tourism without a visa arranged in advance, typically for stays of up to 90 days. Requirements and any associated fees vary by nationality and have changed before, so confirm yours with Argentina's official immigration source ahead of your flight.

**Will Argentine immigration actually ask for my return ticket?**
It's inconsistent. Some travellers are never asked, others are asked directly, and it often comes down to the individual officer. The airline checkpoint at your departure airport is the more consistent one to plan for, since airlines carry liability if they board you without proof you can leave Argentina.

**Can I use a flight reservation instead of a real ticket?**
Yes. What matters is that it's genuine and verifiable, a real PNR that can be looked up in the airline's system, not that you've paid full fare for a ticketed flight. That's exactly what a held reservation provides.

**What if I'm travelling onward overland to Chile, Uruguay, or Brazil?**
A bus or ferry ticket can sometimes satisfy the requirement, but land and river crossings are less standardised than airline check-in procedures. If you're flying into Argentina first, having an onward flight reservation ready is still the safer bet.

**How far in advance should I sort my onward reservation?**
No need to rush it. A held reservation takes minutes to generate, so most travellers sort it a few days before departure, once the rest of the trip is locked in.
