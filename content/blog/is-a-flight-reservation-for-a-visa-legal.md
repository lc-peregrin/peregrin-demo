# Is a Flight Reservation for a Visa Legal? What Official Guidance Says

**Short answer:** yes. A genuine flight reservation, meaning a real booking held with an airline in your name with a booking reference that can be checked, is a legitimate document to put in a visa application. For Schengen visas it is exactly what the EU's own guidance asks for, and it says a paid ticket should not be required when you apply. What is never legal is a made-up or edited document that looks like a booking but isn't one.

## Schengen: a reservation is the standard, a paid ticket is not required

The European Commission's handbook for consulates processing Schengen visas lists, among the supporting documents an applicant can provide, a "reservation of or return or round ticket". It goes further and tells consulates that "a paid return ticket should not be required upon lodging" an application, though one may be asked for in exceptional cases once the visa has been issued. ([Visa Code Handbook I, European Commission, 2024](https://www.nyidanmark.dk/-/media/Files/US/Lovstof/Visumkodeks/Visumhaandbogen/Visa-Code-Handbook-I-2024-EN-Annex.pdf), copy published by the Danish Immigration Service.)

Individual countries say the same thing in their own checklists:

- **The Netherlands** tells tourist applicants they need "a reservation in your name for your travel to and from the Schengen area" and that "you do not need a paid travel ticket." ([Netherlands Worldwide, Schengen visa checklist for tourism](https://www.netherlandsworldwide.nl/visa-the-netherlands/checklist-schengen-visa-tourism))
- **Germany** lists a "flight reservation" as a required document and adds that "a booked flight ticket does not result in a preferred processing of the application." ([German Missions in India, Schengen visa checklist for tourism](https://india.diplo.de/in-en/-/2674158))

So for a Schengen visa, a reservation is not a workaround. It is the document the rules expect.

## United Kingdom: no flight booking needed, and don't book before a decision

The UK is different, and it is worth being clear about it. GOV.UK advises: "You should not book your travel until you get a decision on your visa application." ([GOV.UK, how to apply for a visa](https://www.gov.uk/apply-uk-visa/standard-visa-form)) Its guide to supporting documents for visitors lists "flight bookings (unless transiting)" among documents that are less useful as evidence. ([GOV.UK, visiting the UK: guide to supporting documents](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/visitor-visa-guide-to-supporting-documents/guide-to-supporting-documents-visiting-the-uk))

In plain terms: for a UK Standard Visitor visa you don't need to show a flight booking at all. A reservation isn't wrong, but it won't strengthen the application, so don't buy anything for it.

## United States and Canada

Neither the US nor Canada asks for a flight booking as part of a standard visitor visa application. Canada's immigration department points out that you "can't travel to Canada until we put the visa in the passport you submit to us," which is a good reason not to buy a ticket early. ([IRCC, after you apply for a visitor visa](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/visit-canada/after-apply-next-steps.html)) For the US, check the State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) for its current advice on travel plans before you apply.

## Proof of onward travel at the border

Separately from visa applications, many countries make a return or onward ticket a condition of entry for visitors, and airlines check it before boarding. Two official examples:

- **The Philippines:** visa-free visitors "must also possess return tickets to their country of origin or outward-bound tickets to their next country." ([eVisa Philippines, official portal](https://evisa.gov.ph/page/policy))
- **Indonesia:** both visa-free entry and the e-VOA list a "return ticket or onward ticket to continue traveling to another country." ([Directorate General of Immigration, visa FAQ](https://www.imigrasi.go.id/faq/visa?lang=en-US))

Here, a verifiable reservation that leaves within your permitted stay is what check-in agents and officers look for: a real booking they can look up.

## Where the line is

The legal question is never "reservation or ticket". It is "real or fake".

- **Legitimate:** a genuine reservation held with the airline in your name, with a booking reference you can look up on the airline's own website. This is what Peregrin provides.
- **Legitimate:** a refundable ticket you later cancel, or a real onward flight you intend to take.
- **Not legitimate:** a document generated or edited to look like a booking, with nothing behind it in the airline's system. On a visa application that is a false document, and it can lead to refusal and a record that follows you into future applications.

A quick test before you use anything: enter the booking reference on the airline's "manage booking" page. If your name and flight come up, it's real.

## FAQ

**Is it legal to use a flight reservation instead of a ticket for a Schengen visa?**
Yes. The European Commission's guidance to consulates lists a flight reservation as a supporting document and says a paid ticket should not be required when you apply. Several member states, including the Netherlands and Germany, say the same in their own checklists.

**Do I need a flight booking for a UK visitor visa?**
No. GOV.UK advises against booking travel before you get a decision and lists flight bookings among documents that are less useful as evidence for visitors.

**Is a "dummy ticket" legal?**
It depends on what it is. If it's a real reservation held with the airline that can be checked, yes. If it's a made-up or edited document, no: that is a false document.

**Will an embassy check my reservation?**
They can, and some do. A genuine reservation can be looked up with the airline using its booking reference, which is why a real one is the only safe choice.

**Does a reservation commit me to the flight?**
No. A held reservation is not a paid ticket. It lapses under the fare's own terms unless you choose to pay for the flight.
