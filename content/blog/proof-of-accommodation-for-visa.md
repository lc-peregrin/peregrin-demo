# Proof of Accommodation for a Visa: What You Actually Need

You've got the flights sorted, or you're about to. Then the visa checklist throws you a second curveball: proof of accommodation. Somewhere to stay, documented, dated, and ready to show a consular officer or a border agent who's never met you and isn't in the mood to guess.

It trips people up more than it should, mostly because "proof of accommodation" means slightly different things depending on which country you're applying to and what kind of visa you're after. Let's sort out what it actually means, who's asking for it, and how to satisfy it without overpaying or overcommitting.

## What "proof of accommodation" actually means

At its simplest, proof of accommodation is a document showing where you'll be staying during your trip and for how long. In practice it usually takes one of three forms:

1. **A confirmed hotel or lodging booking.** A reservation with your name, dates, and address, from a hotel, guesthouse, or short-term rental.
2. **An invitation or host letter.** If you're staying with family, friends, or a local contact, a signed letter from them (often with a copy of their ID and proof they're allowed to host you) confirming you have a place to stay.
3. **A rental agreement or lease.** Less common for short trips, more relevant if you're relocating or on a longer-stay visa.

Here's the part worth saying plainly: requirements genuinely vary by consulate, by visa category, and by your nationality. Some embassies want a full itinerary with a hotel booked for every single night. Others barely glance at it as long as something's on file. There's no universal rulebook here, so treat anything you read online, including this article, as a starting point and always check the specific consulate's or embassy's official guidance before you submit anything.

## Who asks for this, and why

Immigration authorities want reasonable confidence that you have somewhere to actually stay, that your trip has a real shape to it, and that you're not going to show up with no plan and become their problem. It's less about grilling you and more about basic due diligence.

Schengen visa applications are the classic example. Most Schengen consulates ask for accommodation details covering your whole stay, alongside proof of onward or return travel and enough funds to cover yourself. But this pattern shows up well beyond Europe too. Plenty of tourist visas, e-visas, and even some visa-on-arrival processes ask the same question in one form or another: where exactly are you sleeping, and can you prove it?

## The "do I have to prepay everything" worry

This is the bit that stresses people out, and understandably so. Nobody wants to pay in full for three weeks of hotels before they even know their visa's approved.

The good news: most consulates don't require a prepaid, non-refundable booking. A confirmed reservation is usually enough, and a free-to-cancel one is often perfectly acceptable, since what they're checking is that a real booking exists in your name for the right dates, not that you've already handed over the cash. That said, "usually" is doing some work in that sentence. A handful of consulates and visa categories do want to see a paid booking, so this is exactly the kind of detail to verify against the specific country's official visa instructions before you commit to a strategy.

## How to satisfy it, ranked by what actually works

**1. A confirmed, free-to-cancel hotel booking.**
This is the go-to for most travellers and most visa types. Book something through [Booking.com](#), filter for free cancellation, and you've got a real, verifiable reservation with your name and dates on it, without locking in money you might not get back. If your plans shift or the visa takes longer than expected, you cancel and rebook. No harm done.

**2. An invitation or host letter.**
If you're staying with someone, this is often the correct and expected route rather than a workaround. Get the letter in writing, signed, with the host's full name, address, and ID details, and check whether the consulate wants it notarised or accompanied by proof of the host's own residency status. Requirements here vary a lot by country, so check the specifics.

**3. A paid booking, if the consulate specifically demands it.**
Some do. If official guidance says a refundable or free-to-cancel booking won't be accepted, don't try to outsmart the requirement, just book what's asked for and build the cost into your trip budget.

One thing that should never be on this list: submitting a booking that's been edited, faked, or doctored to look confirmed when it isn't. It doesn't just risk the visa, it risks your ability to apply again in future, and it falls apart the moment anyone actually verifies it against the hotel's own system. A real, cancellable booking costs very little and takes minutes. There's no good reason to gamble with a fake one.

## Before you apply: a quick checklist

- Check the specific consulate's or embassy's official page for exactly what "proof of accommodation" means for your visa type. Don't rely on forum posts from three years ago.
- Book accommodation that covers your full stated stay, or as much of it as the guidance requires.
- Choose free cancellation where it's allowed, so you're not out of pocket if plans change.
- Sort your onward or return travel proof at the same time, since most applications want both.
- Print or save digital copies of everything. Some consulates want printed originals, others are fine with PDFs.
- If you're staying with a host, get the invitation letter early. These can take a few days to arrange properly.
- Double check names, dates, and spellings match your passport and visa application exactly. Small mismatches cause unnecessary back-and-forth.

## FAQ

**Do I have to pay for the whole stay upfront?**
Usually not. Most consulates accept a confirmed reservation without requiring full prepayment, and free-to-cancel bookings are commonly fine. Some do want a paid booking, so check the specific requirements for your visa and country before you decide.

**Can I use a booking I'm planning to cancel?**
Generally yes, as long as it's a genuine, confirmed reservation at the time you submit it and the consulate hasn't specified otherwise. What matters is that it's real and verifiable when you apply, not that you're locked into it forever. Just don't submit something you've already cancelled or edited to look active.

**What if I'm staying with friends or family instead of a hotel?**
An invitation or host letter is usually the right document. It typically needs the host's details and sometimes proof of their own residency or ID. Check what your specific consulate expects, since the format requirements differ.

**Does proof of accommodation replace proof of onward travel?**
No, they're usually separate requirements asked for together. Accommodation shows where you're staying, onward or return travel proof shows you're leaving when you're supposed to. Most applications want both.

**What happens if my accommodation plans change after I get the visa?**
In most cases, that's fine. The booking was to demonstrate you had a genuine plan at the time of application, not to lock your entire trip in place. If you're unsure, the consulate's own guidance or a quick check with your visa application centre is the safest way to confirm.
